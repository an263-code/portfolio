## How to use this

This is not a deck, and it is not a script. It is the **argument** the pitch has to make, in the order it has to make it, grounded in the patterns the 20-customer ICP analysis surfaced. Sales decks, discovery flows, demo storylines, and landing-page copy should all flatten down to this structure.

The pitch only works if the buyer accepts step 1. If they don't feel the shift, steps 2–8 sound like a vendor bragging about its features. Spend real time on step 1.

## 1. The Market Insight — The rented-audience era is ending

For two decades, consumer research ran on a simple bargain: buy panel access, send a survey, extract an answer. That bargain is breaking.

Response rates on cold-email surveys have halved in three years. Third-party-hosted panels churn faster than they're topped up. Members tell you directly — in the comments, in the opt-outs — that they've had enough. Privacy expectations have shifted in the same direction: people want to know who they're talking to and why.

At the same time, what leadership is asking for from the research function has moved in the opposite direction. CEOs and CMOs walking into brand-led enterprises are mandating new revenue streams, direct-to-consumer motions, loyalty programs, and "one-brand" operating models — all of which require a *relationship* with a defined audience, not a census of strangers. They want always-on, mixed-method intelligence about customers the brand directly owns.

**The shift:** customer intelligence is moving from transactional extraction to relationship-based participation. The brands that win the next decade will **own their audience, not rent it**.

> *"We've had to send more and more surveys every year to get the same engagement… our response rate has about cut in half."* — Danielle Lebowitz, Royal Caribbean
>
> *"Our CEO joined last year… each of the brands have now got three revenue streams, where in the past it was just one, which was advertising… hence the change in direction."* — Bhavesh Vekaria, Hearst UK

## 2. The Alternatives — What insights teams are actually doing today

When a research or insights leader tries to meet this new mandate with what they already have, they're operating on some combination of:

- **A patchwork of point tools** — Qualtrics for the brand tracker, Decipher for custom surveys, UserTesting for usability, Medallia for CX, a separate video vendor, a separate polling tool for advocacy or comms. Each owned by a different team. None integrated.
- **A rented or third-party-hosted "panel"** that the team pays to access but doesn't actually own, brand, or control.
- **Per-study list pulls from marketing or CRM**, with no persistent opted-in audience. Every study starts from scratch.
- **Episodic, project-by-project research** with the same two or three researchers doing programming, recruit, moderation, and analysis end-to-end.
- **DIY community attempts** spun up on adjacent software without operational support — which burn out the team running them.
- **Doing nothing** — stakeholders either stop asking, or they procure their own shadow point tools and fragment the function further.

---

## 3. The Flaws — Why these alternatives buckle under the new mandate

None of these alternatives are "wrong." They were built for the world that's ending. In the world that's arriving, they fail in structurally predictable ways.

- **Fragmentation erodes governance.** When every team owns its own tool, evidence is scattered across four reporting environments and no one can cross-reference. The insights function loses the ability to shape enterprise-level understanding of the customer — which is the exact thing leadership is now demanding.
- **Rented audiences degrade silently.** Response rates fall, sample targets require larger and larger sends, and the team has no way to tell leadership *why* the signal is getting noisier. The research still ships; the evidence underneath it quietly stops being representative.
- **Episodic research caps the team at its own delivery rate.** Every study eats the week. Nothing compounds. A backlog forms that the team openly admits they will never clear.
- **Recruit-per-study economics kill mixed methods.** Qualitative becomes a separate pool, a separate recruit, a separate cost — so triangulated research becomes a budget event instead of a default. Video, diary studies, and concept testing stay rare.
- **Small teams can't operate always-on infrastructure alone.** A two-to-ten-person insights team servicing a growing enterprise mandate cannot also run a living community on the side. The ones who try without help churn out.

The deepest flaw is not any single one of these — it's that none of the alternatives give the research function **organisational leverage**. They let the team work harder. They don't let the team work *bigger*.

> *"Our advocacy team does their own polling with a different partner. Our web team uses user testing… I would love to set something up that can be utilized by different parts of the organization."* — Brian Cancian, Feeding America

## 4. The Map of the Land — What a winning solution has to include

If you've decided the status quo is untenable, the solution you adopt must deliver **four things together**. Miss any one and you've rebuilt the old stack with a new logo on it.

1. **An owned, branded, always-on community.** Not a panel. Not a list. A persistent, opted-in, member-first audience that carries your brand — one you engage continuously, not per project.
2. **Mixed methods against one member pool.** Survey, quick poll, diary study, video, concept testing, and usability research all reaching the same people — so insight triangulates instead of fragmenting, and recruit cost collapses.
3. **A managed-service partnership that runs it with you.** Not software you'll figure out. People who handle community engagement, recruit, moderation, programming, and incentive fulfilment — so your team does the work only they can do.
4. **A research-function-led hub that stakeholders can self-serve against.** Consolidation that gives the insights team governance and gives non-researcher stakeholders (product, marketing, brand, loyalty) curated access without a queue.

This is the point of view. If a vendor conversation ignores any of the four, the buyer is being sold last decade's stack.

## 5. The Perfect World — What this unlocks

When the research team stops being a custom-order pipeline and becomes the orchestrator of a living community, the function changes shape:

- Stakeholders get answers in days, not quarters — because the audience is already there.
- The team shifts from *doers of studies* to *curators of a capability*. Its value is no longer capped by its own delivery rate.
- Mixed-method studies become routine. A survey leads to a video diary leads to a usability test against the same members — no extra recruits, no extra budget battles.
- Leadership can ask a question this week and get grounded evidence the next. The insights function becomes a competitive advantage, not a service desk.
- The community itself becomes a brand asset — a place members return to, not a list they get emailed from.
- The evidence base compounds. Every study adds to a known, profiled, opted-in audience the team can go back to — instead of starting from zero.

The outcome isn't "faster research." It's a different relationship between the brand, its customers, and the people inside the company who decide what to build, what to say, and where to grow.

> *"The panel support aspect of it was very appealing to us because we don't want to be panel managers. We want to be researchers."* — Danielle Lebowitz, Royal Caribbean

## 7. The Value Pillars

### Pillar 1 — A community you own, always on

The core of Alida is a branded, opted-in community that members return to. Your domain, your brand, your governance, your relationship. Surveys and polls become a reason for members to show up — not a reason to unsubscribe.

Because the community is a *destination* rather than a *list*, it scales: 7-Eleven operates a 150,000-member community where a single post can generate 6,000 comments. Royal Caribbean replaced a declining cold-email motion with an opted-in guest community. Amazon UK stood up a long-horizon member community tied directly to Private Brands' knowledge gaps.

This is the pillar that replaces the rented-panel era with something durable.

### Pillar 2 — Every method, one member pool

A member recruited through a survey, followed up in a video diary, re-contacted for a usability test a month later, and pulled into a concept test a quarter after that is worth an order of magnitude more than four separate recruits.

Alida runs survey, quick poll, diary study, moderated and unmoderated video, concept testing, and usability research against the same member pool — with AI summarisation pulling open-ends, transcripts, and themes into one analysis surface. Sony Pictures uses it for content testing with watermarking and NDA flows. Feeding America uses it to generate donor-story highlight reels alongside quantitative segmentation. Comcast Cable layers qualitative directly onto its existing Decipher quant motion.

This is the pillar that makes mixed-method research economical instead of aspirational.

### Pillar 3 — A partner who runs it with you

Your team is two to ten people. They cannot absorb a new platform they operate alone — and you know it. Alida's managed-service tiers (Premier Community Management, managed recruit, moderation, programming, incentive fulfilment) absorb the operational load so your team does the work only they can do: interpret evidence and shape decisions.

ALSAC chose "do-it-for-me" in the first phase explicitly: *"in the first phase, it is always going to be do it for me."* FordDirect, when forced to pick between video and community management, picked community management. When Hearst UK hit a significant reporting gap mid-deployment, the deal held because the CSM and VP-level partnership held.

This is the pillar the rest of the industry treats as an add-on. Alida treats it as the product.

## Guardrails — how this pitch goes wrong

Three failure modes recur across the loss set and are worth naming so the team can spot them in real time.

1. **Leading with features instead of the shift.** If step 1 doesn't land, every subsequent step sounds like a vendor brag. When a buyer opens with "tell me what you do," resist the urge to demo — re-establish the shift first.
2. **Letting the evaluation become a capability bake-off.** The moment the conversation reduces to a side-by-side grid against a cheaper point tool, Alida's three pillars become invisible and the deal defaults to price. The response is escalation to a partnership-depth conversation with the sponsor — not a discount.
3. **Selling the managed-service tier as an add-on line item.** For the ICP, it is the product. Treating it as an optional SKU signals that Alida has misunderstood its own value, and it lets finance re-scope the deal down to "just the software" — at which point the buyer is no longer buying what Alida sells.
