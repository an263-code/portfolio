Trackers Drift. Dashboards Can't Trend Them.

Researchers run tracker programs as a series of separate surveys — a new one every wave. Between waves, question names and answer scales drift. The same underlying question comes back wearing a different shape, and Modern Dashboards had no way to chart it as one metric.

One question, three shapes

Three waves, three shapes, one underlying question. One reportable trend line? Not without a mapping layer.

So customers built the trend themselves — exporting each wave and stitching results together in spreadsheets. The analysis was happening outside the product, which is the clearest signal a reporting tool can get.

There was a second, quieter problem — a process one. A cross-source mapping experience is interaction-heavy, and interaction-heavy is exactly what static Figma mocks are worst at. Each hi-fi direction costs weeks to build, so most directions never get built, and the ones that do get judged on pictures rather than behavior. That constraint shaped everything I did next.

The charter

  1. Design the feature — cross-source field mapping that sits naturally alongside the tools researchers already use: Recodes, Weighting, Banners, Filters.
  2. Validate before build — evidence from real researchers on a working prototype, not opinions on pictures.
  3. Hand off unambiguously — a spec engineering can click through, inside the quarter.

None of it was solo work. PM and engineering were in design crits throughout, and every iteration below was reviewed cross-functionally.

Weeks per direction isn't a schedule problem. It's a design constraint.

Six Working Prototypes in One Week

I built six prototypes with Claude Code in a week. Every one is real, clickable HTML — not a mock — so design crits with PM and engineering ran on working software, and feedback turned around the same day.

  1. v1 · Dialog chooser — wizard plus AI auto-detect, following the existing Recodes pattern
  2. v2 · Inline choice — the choice moves onto the page; one less dialog
  3. v3 · Living rules — the pivot: a grid where the rows are the mapping rules
  4. v4 · One page + modal — column-scoped AI detection; the grid grows both ways
  5. v5.2 · Manual grid — AI stripped out to validate the foundation. Team pick, and the tested build
  6. v6 · Single-select — a strict 1:1 alternative, documented as a trade-off
1 / 6

The reset is the point, not the count

v1 through v4 all led with AI auto-detection. In crit after crit, the same question kept coming back: would researchers actually trust a mapping the system made for them? Nobody could answer it, because nothing we had put in front of anyone separated the two questions — does the interaction model work, and does the AI earn its place.

In the old workflow, that feedback goes into a backlog and dies there, because starting over costs weeks. Here each direction cost about a day. So v5 could be a full reset rather than a patch: strip every AI suggestion out, and validate the manual foundation on its own first. v5.2 became the team pick and the build we tested. v6 documented the strict single-select alternative so the trade-off was written down, not just argued.

AI made being wrong cheap — which is what made the reset affordable.

v5.2 — A Grid That Teaches Its Own Structure

Key Decision 1

Rows are rules, columns are sources

The structure carries the explanation. Each row is one mapped field; each column is one data source. Growth affordances sit on the edges they grow from — add a field at the bottom, add a source on the right — so the grid teaches its own model without a wizard walking anyone through it.

Key Decision 2

A rule plus a materialized field

Each mapping saves two things: the rule and the result. Dashboards read the stored field, so they stay fast — and when the next wave lands, the rule extends the field automatically. No rebuild, no re-mapping, no stale trend line.

Key Decision 3

Manual-first, on purpose

v5 stripped out every AI suggestion — not because AI didn't belong, but because a test of the grid and a test of the AI are two different tests. Leaving auto-detection in would have told us how people felt about the suggestions, and nothing about whether the underlying interaction model holds.

Key Decision 4

Values align by direct manipulation

Behind each row's chevron sits the harder half of the problem: three sources whose answer options don't line up. A drag-and-drop matrix merges source options into one reportable value — "Yes" and "Definitely" and the top two scale points land in the same bucket, visibly, by hand. This is the interaction users called out in testing.

Tested on Our Own Platform, Demoed to Customers

We validated two ways at once, and both ran on Alida itself.

An unmoderated usability test — dogfooding Alida end to end

Distribution was the real blocker, and it took one prompt to clear. A local HTML file can't reach participants; pasting the prototype into Figma Make produced a published URL we could put inside a survey. The rest of the pipeline was our own product doing its job on us.

The study itself: two realistic tasks — map a drifted repurchase question across three waves, then cover a newly closed Q4 wave and save. We measured with SUS plus open probes, with screen and click recording and AI follow-up probes on the open answers.

And we pre-committed the decision rules before fielding, so the results would produce a decision rather than a debate.

"If at least half of participants say manual-only mapping won't scale to their programs, v5 needs an AI-assist layer."
Written into the test plan before the study was fielded

Live prototype demos on customer calls

In parallel, I joined customer calls and drove the prototype live. Customers watched every interaction actually happen — expanding a row, dragging values into a merge, saving — instead of being walked through screenshots. That surfaced the kind of question pictures never provoke: "what happens when the scales disagree?"

Same artifact, three jobs: crit material, test stimulus, demo.

The Grid Works — and Told Us Exactly What's Next

84 SUS — Grade A, against a benchmark average of 68
5 / 5 Completed both tasks, in ~4.3 minutes on average
5 / 5 Would use it frequently — top-two-box agreement
0 / 5 Want AI to decide alone — everyone wants review control

n = 5 against a recruit target of 12–20, so these results are directional; task completion is self-reported.

The interaction model works; the manual-first premise doesn't scale.

Four of five said hand-building mappings is viable only for a few key questions — crossing the decision rule we'd set before fielding. So the roadmap moved on evidence, not preference: v5 needs an AI-assist layer, and we know it because we said in advance what would prove it.

"It was a nice surprise to be able to map multiple source values to the same output value via the drag and drop."
P3 — SUS 97.5
"12 monthly surveys with 20 questions each would be painfully slow to set up manually."

The same participant who scored the prototype highest is the one who explained why AI drafting has to come back — the interaction is good, the volume is the problem.

Handoff: A Spec Engineers Click, Not Read

The spec

The coded v5.2 is the interaction spec

Engineering received the working prototype. Every behavior is something they click through, not something they decode from annotations — and hi-fi mocks now only pin down visual detail. No wordy annotation documents to write, or to misread.

Already shipped

Test findings shipped before handoff

Two findings from the study went into v5.2 before it went out: source-column reordering, and a "Not mapped" tray so values removed from a mapping are parked rather than lost.

The roadmap

A roadmap set by evidence

Next is AI-drafted field and value matching layered over the validated grid — manual becomes the fallback, not the default. The order matters: we know the foundation holds, because we tested it without the AI on top of it.

The delta

The efficiency delta

Six explored directions in about a week, against weeks per direction in static mocks — with one artifact serving crits, the usability test, and customer demos.

Reflection

Speed buys judgment.

Cheap iteration isn't about drawing faster. What it actually bought was the v5 reset — throwing away four directions and re-testing the premise underneath them, which no schedule would have allowed at weeks per direction. When building a direction stops being the expensive part, deciding what to test becomes the craft.

Evidence beats debate. Pre-committing decision rules turned "should AI assist here?" from an opinion war into a measurement. Four of five crossed the line we'd drawn in advance, and the argument was over. I'll write those rules into every study now.

Trust is the design problem. Users and customers converged on the same principle from opposite directions — zero of five wanted AI deciding on its own, and customers kept asking how they'd verify a mapping when the scales disagree. AI drafts; humans review. That's the product principle this feature is built on.

What's next: design the AI-drafting layer so suggestions arrive as reviewable drafts and never as silent decisions; close the confidence gap with response counts per mapped value and a live data preview, so users can check they got it right; and re-test at proper scale — the 12–20 participants the original plan called for, with the per-task measures we cut.
